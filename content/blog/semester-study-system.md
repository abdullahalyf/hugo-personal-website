Every semester I start with the same promise: this time I will use a proper
system, with spaced repetition and a calendar and a productivity app. This
time I did not. What I used instead is a notebook, a deck of index cards and
one recurring Sunday afternoon. Two years in, it still works, so here is what
it actually is.

## The notebook is the source of truth

One notebook for the semester, dated at the top of every page. Not one per
subject — that is the mistake I made twice. Subjects interleave, and separate
notebooks make me lose the thread when a question in one topic depends on
something from another.

Each page holds:

- the lecture in my own words, not slides, because I cannot copy slides fast
  enough to be useful
- the example that made it click, if there was one
- a question mark in the margin wherever I did not understand something

The margin question marks are the important part. They are the only thing in my
notes that I always go back to.

## Cards for the things I keep forgetting

Not everything deserves a card. A card is worth making if I got it right on one
day and wrong on the next, which usually means the idea is specific rather than
vague. "How does a hash map work" does not become a card. "Why does a hash map
need to resize" does.

One side is the question, the other is my answer written in one sentence. If I
cannot write the answer in one sentence, the question is too big and becomes a
notebook page instead.

## The Sunday review

Every Sunday evening, same time, roughly two hours:

1. Read the margin question marks from the week and either answer them in the
   notebook or write down that I still cannot answer them.
2. Shuffle the cards and test myself on each one. Cards I get wrong go to the
   front of the next week's pile. Cards I get right twice in a row leave the
   pile.
3. Look at what the week actually covered and adjust next week's reading.

> The review is the system. Without it, the cards and the notebook are just
> things I own.

## The list that runs the week

I keep a short list of open loops. It is the only list I check every morning.

- [x] Re-read the hash map resize section (from cards, 12 March)
- [x] Solve three problems on amortised cost (from notebook, page 88)
- [ ] Rewrite the TCP handshake notes, they are wrong
- [ ] Ask about the second question in the Thursday assignment

The point of the list is that it is short enough to hold in my head. When it
grew past fifteen items I stopped trusting it, so I moved everything else into
the notebook where it stopped bothering me.

## What it does not do

It does not schedule. I do not plan a whole semester in January. I plan two
weeks at a time, because a two-week plan survives a bad week and a semester
plan does not.

It also does not track time. I tried a stopwatch for a month and it made me
anxious about my numbers rather than my understanding. The Sunday review tells
me if I am on track without turning every evening into a report.

## What I would change

If I started again I would keep a running list of questions per topic, not
scattered through margins, because finding them again takes time. I would also
write the card answers in the exact words of the textbook or the teacher. My
own phrasing hides the gaps — I can restate something wrongly and not notice.

Two semesters in, the system is not impressive, but I am passing, and I know
which parts I do not actually understand. That is the whole goal.
